The Brundtland Report (1987), widely regarded as the founding document of the field, marks the starting point of a discourse that has continuously provided material for the study of specialised discourse, corpora and phraseology. This constantly evolving field (Zaccaï, 2011) has been carried forward by the Rio summits (1992, 2012), Agenda 21 and subsequently the 2030 Agenda, which have institutionalised a dense body of texts produced by a wide range of stakeholders and across highly diverse discourse genres: progress reports, conventions, national plans, assessment reports, blog posts and social media content, issued by international organisations, governments, industry, NGOs, associations, and others. Sustainable development discourse cannot, therefore, be reduced to a discourse conveyed solely by expert speakers, with a stabilised terminology and fixed text genres (Lerat, 1995; Cabré, 1998; L’Homme, 2004). On the contrary, it is characterised by constant porosity between its various enunciators – from experts and policy-makers to journalists, activists and the general public – with its units shifting from one register to another without their meaning ever becoming definitively fixed.

The abundance of material and stakeholders in the field contrasts, however, with a poor coordination of the methods used to analyse sustainable development. One of the main methods for exploiting sustainable development data, environmental assessment, relies primarily on life cycle assessment, standardised by ISO 14040 and 14044, which quantifies the impacts of a product from resource extraction to end of life, yet makes little and unsystematic use of textual data. Sustainable development corpora, however, constitute a rich resource and a privileged observatory for the study of the field, its terminology and its specialised phraseology (Bowker & Pearson, 2002; Condamines, 2005). Fine “granularity” (Mejri, 2026c) is central here: a general concept branches out into a multitude of sub-themes, whose degree of granularity varies according to the specificity of the objects or events concerned. *Pollution*, for instance, is broken down into *marine pollution*, *air pollution*, *soil pollution*, and so on. This branching multiplies multiword units and allows the key concepts and objects of the field to be targeted more precisely. It is therefore incumbent upon linguists, lexicographers, and corpus and artificial intelligence specialists to take up this timely field of research, which constantly underlines both the urgency and the complexity of the domain, as well as the scientific value and societal impact of such a research project.

Following a first study day held on 9 June 2026 at the ALTAE laboratory and the UFR EILA of Université Paris Cité on “Dictionaries and sustainable development discourse”, this second event focuses on the interplay between sustainable development, corpora and artificial intelligence.

## Strand 1 – Corpus design and tools

A corpus is not a neutral artefact: it is a methodological construct whose sampling and filtering criteria shape the results (Biber, 1993; Habert, Nazarenko & Salem, 1997). In a field as heterogeneous as sustainable development, which sources should be included or excluded, and which degree of specialisation and which register should be taken into account? These theoretical choices are compounded by technical constraints that are rarely made explicit: heterogeneous distribution formats, loss and noise introduced during conversion, instability and inaccessibility of online sources, imbalances in data volume across stakeholders and languages, and so on. Each processing pipeline thus results from a series of trade-offs between comprehensiveness and quality, and between automation and manual control.

Contributions to this strand may address:

- criteria for designing heterogeneous sustainable development corpora;
- metadata architecture, source traceability, documentation of processing pipelines, reproducibility and data management plans;
- comparable or parallel multilingual corpora, and the handling of imbalances across languages and text genres;
- lexicometric tools for the inductive exploration of sustainable development discourse;
- legal and ethical issues (data access rights, personal data, anonymisation);
- etc.

## Strand 2 – Lexicographic, phraseological and terminological resources

This second strand addresses the lexicographic, phraseological and terminological resources that make it possible to account for a lexicon as dynamic as that of sustainable development. Papers will extend existing work on linguistic resources, phraseological units, collocations and discourse formulae, as well as on semantic and predicative relations in specialised language production (Gross, 1981; Mel’čuk, Clas & Polguère, 1995; Granger & Meunier, eds., 2008; Mejri, Meneses-Lerín & Buffard-Moret, 2020).

Proposals may address:

- the use of lexicographic resources in the linguistic description of domain data;
- criteria for delimiting collocations and phraseological units;
- the construction of semantic networks specific to sustainable development;
- the construction of user-oriented multilingual terminological databases (for translators, experts, policy-makers, etc.);
- the interplay between expert resources and corpus-derived resources;
- etc.

## Strand 3 – Experimenting with AI

The third strand focuses on experimentation. Large language models (LLMs) are transforming the research landscape (Brown *et al.*, 2020; Pecman, 2025; Mejri, 2026b; Zhu, 2026), particularly for the extraction of terms, phraseological units and collocations from specialised corpora, without, however, removing the need for linguistic validation.

Papers on the following topics are particularly welcome:

- automatic extraction of terms and phraseological units;
- extraction of multiword neologisms, detection of emerging concepts and structuring of semantic fields from sustainable development discourse;
- methods for the co-construction of prompts and RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) based on textual or lexicographic resources of the domain;
- evaluation of AI-based methods and their results;
- etc.

## Epistemological and theoretical issues

These three strands also give rise to epistemological and theoretical questions. Expected theoretical outcomes include:

- the *moule-matrice* (matrix-mould) as a mechanism of perpetual motion between fixation and the generation of combinatorics and language production (Mejri, 2025; Zhu, 2022, 2026);
- the semantic and inferential network of specialised discourse, in both its onomasiological and its predicative dimensions (Pecman, 2007; Mejri & Zhu, 2020; Mejri, 2026a);
- thematic granularity as a filter and classifier of specialised discourse, and as a descriptor of its structure and variation (Kübler & Pecman, 2012; Mejri, 2026c);
- the epistemological status of AI-generated data: what is validated, by whom, and according to which linguistic and reproducibility criteria (Mejri, 2026b; Pecman, 2025; Raus & Tonti, 2025; Zhu, 2026);
- etc.
